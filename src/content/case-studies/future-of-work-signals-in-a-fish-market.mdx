Some of the earliest signals of change do not appear in market reports, dashboards or strategy presentations. They appear in everyday life, if we know how to observe them.

## The observation

During an early morning walk along Marina Beach in Chennai, a visit to a local fish market revealed something striking.

People were negotiating prices, cleaning fish, organising the morning catch and getting ready for the day's sales. The market was busy and working as it presumably had for generations.

But one detail stood out.

Almost everyone involved had grey or white hair.

There were very few young people.

What looked at first like a simple demographic observation raised a much larger question. Were we looking at an ageing workforce, or at the early signs of an occupation slowly disappearing?

## Looking beyond the obvious

An ageing workforce can be more than a human-resources issue. It can be a leading indicator of deeper structural change.

If younger generations are no longer going into traditional fishing, fish trading and related work, the consequences may reach well beyond the people involved. Over time, this could affect:

- Local supply chains
- Independent entrepreneurship
- Community resilience
- How knowledge passes from one generation to the next
- Consumers' access to fresh, locally sourced food
- The structure and ownership of an entire economic ecosystem

Technology, modern retail and organised supply chains bring enormous gains in efficiency, scale and convenience. But transformation rarely happens without displacement.

As independent livelihoods decline, economies may shift towards formal employment.

As traditional markets disappear, organised supply chains may take their place.

As knowledge once handed down from one generation to the next fades, systems, processes and institutional expertise may replace it.

The important question is not whether these changes are inherently good or bad. It is this: what else changes when they happen?

## Questions hidden inside an ordinary marketplace

What we saw that morning at Marina Beach raises several strategic questions about the future of work.

- Will future generations keep choosing occupations that involve physical hardship, uncertainty and risk?
- Could fishing and similar traditional livelihoods increasingly become organised industries dominated by larger enterprises?
- If some of these occupations are not profitable enough for large businesses, who will preserve the skills and knowledge that go with them?
- What happens when an occupation disappears before its knowledge has been passed on?
- Who will own essential skills in future: communities, individuals or corporations?
- What happens to economic resilience as local ecosystems become more centralised?
- Which new businesses will emerge from this transition, and which existing ecosystems may quietly disappear?

## Efficiency is not the same as resilience

Businesses are trained to measure efficiency.

But societies also depend on resilience.

The two are not always the same.

A highly centralised supply chain may be more efficient under normal conditions. A distributed ecosystem of small producers, traders and local markets may offer a different kind of resilience. The same tension runs through many traditional occupations.

So the bigger question may not simply be whether technology will replace traditional work. It may be whether we are slowly creating economies in which fewer people are able, or willing, to build independent livelihoods, and more of them depend on formal organisations for employment.

That shift can have profound implications for businesses, policymakers, communities and consumers.

## The anthropological lens

Anthropology teaches us to pay attention to [ordinary behaviour](/glossary/behavioural-research/). A market, a neighbourhood, a workplace or a conversation can reveal social and economic change long before it becomes visible in quantitative data.

For businesses, this kind of observation can surface questions that conventional market research may not yet be asking.

The most important questions about the future of work, communities and culture do not always emerge from a boardroom. They can emerge from a quiet morning walk through a local market.

## The strategic question

As leaders, we often ask: what will the future of work look like?

Perhaps we should also ask: what, and who, might we lose along the way?

Are we witnessing progress, or the gradual disappearance of livelihoods that have sustained communities for generations?

Sometimes, tomorrow's biggest business and societal shifts are already visible in today's ordinary moments. We simply have to notice them.
